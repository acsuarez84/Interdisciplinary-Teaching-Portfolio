A Note on Making as Argument

Before this assignment starts, you already know that making something can be a form of scholarly argument, not just a decoration. This came from Unit 3's short reading and discussion on “critical making,” alongside the pattern-finding work in your prompt journal.

Reading: Ratto, “Critical Making: Conceptual and Material Studies in Technology and Social Life,” The Information Society 27, no. 4 (2011): 252–260. tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01972243.2011.583819

This sits behind the publisher's normal access wall — use your library's off-campus login if it doesn't load directly. A freely readable copy is also available via ResearchGate and Academia.edu if you search the title. It gave you two things: the words to explain why the way something is made can carry meaning, and one low-stakes practice round — a small “argument sketch” in a medium of your choice.

The Role of AI in This Assignment

All semester, you have critiqued AI for how it affects and shapes identity. This assignment does not let you avoid AI completely — but it also does not require you to use it yourself.

“Do you use AI or not” is actually two independent decisions. Neither choice affects your grade.

Which systems you can use

The rules for using AI to brainstorm ideas
Every AI-given idea goes into your prompt journal word-for-word, and your reflection must include a note on where each idea came from and why you kept, combined, or dropped it, in your own words.

What Is the Assignment

How does AI shape who we are? Identity Construction Through AI is where you answer that directly — studying a generative AI tool of your choice, focused on 2–3 identity markers, backed by real evidence.

Learning objectives

  • Analysis — Apply feminist and intersectionality frameworks to explain how a generative AI system represents your identity markers, and why that matters.
  • Method — Design and carry out a systematic, documented study of an AI tool — a repeatable practice, not casual prompting.
  • Making — Produce an original artifact that argues something through its form.
  • Transparency — Reflect honestly on the role AI played in your own process.

How the semester built to this

  • Unit 1 — Seeing the design: feminist/intersectionality theories showed AI's design is not neutral.
  • Unit 2 — Testing it on yourself: recorded what happens to your own language and cultural background.
  • Unit 3 — Reading the patterns: flattening, reconstruction, erasure — and that making is real scholarship.
  • Unit 4 — Making the argument: this project, in three parts.

The Work

Part 1 — The Prompt Journal

The foundation this whole assignment is built on. Beginning Week 1: (1) exact prompts submitted, (2) outputs produced, (3) analysis of what changed, was lost, or was rebuilt. Checkpoints due Weeks 5, 9, 13, with real, dated entries. Note: the journal is graded at its three checkpoints (10% each, 300 pts total), not inside this rubric — the criteria below score the artifact and the written reflection only.

If you've been observing instead of prompting AI yourself
That is a complete, equal way of doing this project, not a lesser one. Gather 15+ documented AI outputs from two citable source types (see THE ROLE OF AI IN THIS ASSIGNMENT section). Log source, date, and context for each. Graded on the same rubric.

Part 2 — The Artifact

A creative, multimedia response to your journal. It should make an argument, use its form on purpose, and be understandable outside the classroom.

How this is graded — skill is not the point
None of the options assume prior skill, and none are graded on polish. A hand-drawn zine and a professionally built one can earn the same grade. Workshop time is built into the last two weeks of Unit 4, but the simplest version of any option earns full credit.

A paper artifact — zine, folded book, or craft

Long used in feminist and activist communities to reclaim narratives. This covers any paper artifact you fold, cut, draw or bind — a zine, a lotus fold book, or another physical craft. We build one together in the Week 11 Theory Lab and again in the Week 15 workshop, so you will not be starting from nothing.

Something else — ask me first

If you want a medium that is not one of the two above — a short film, a podcast, a photo essay — email me or book office hours before you start. I will say yes to most things, but I need to know early enough to help you do it well.

Choosing not to use AI
The zine (or any other digital/physical format) can be built entirely without AI tools. The AI artifact option is inherently AI-assisted, by definition. For every other medium, Decision B determines whether AI touches your process. Graded the same regardless of medium.

Part 3 — Written Reflection (500–800 words)

Explains your work as a scholar: the how and why behind your argument, artifact design, and choice of identity marker.

Which identity markers you chose, and why.

The AI tool you studied (or sources gathered) and how you approached it.

Patterns found across your journal, tied to the specific tool and markers.

How your specific feminist/intersectionality framework(s) shaped your analysis — name them.

If you brainstormed with AI: where each idea came from, in your own words.

A full AI disclosure statement, with links and a transcript.

Grading Criteria — Scored Out of 100 (55% of Final Grade)

This is a small, discussion-driven course, so grading here is a flexible, criteria-based rubric rather than a rigid point scale. Each criterion is broken into point bands. The EXAMPLES column below shows the same content that lives behind “Show example›” on the interactive site — here it's printed directly rather than hidden behind a click.

All 12 examples trace one running case study, so the same project is scored differently criterion by criterion: a student auditing how an AI system describes “a nurse” when two identity markers — gender and disability — are combined in the prompt.

These examples are illustrations, not templates
Nothing in the EXAMPLES column — prompts, responses, reflection excerpts, disclosure language — may be copied or submitted as your own work. They show what a point level looks like in practice, not finished material to reuse. Echoing this wording will be treated as not your own work.
The nursing context itself is a different matter: you may study nurses as your real identity-marker topic, but only with prior approval from your professor in Unit 1. That approval never extends to reusing the specific language shown here.
If you chose not to use AI at any stage
Every band applies to you exactly as written — there is no separate, lesser rubric for the alternate paths. Compiled sources are held to the same completeness/citation standard as a prompt log; an AI-free artifact is held to the same argument-and-intentionality standard. Your reflection swaps an AI-use transcript for a complete source list — same honesty bar either way.

Examples From My Own Work

There's no student example bank yet, since this is a new assignment — so here are two of my own projects, made the same way yours will be: an AI audit or observation, followed by a made argument.

The Machine Read the Archive
Movable Twine · AI image audit		Loom of Tongues: Dual Textile Rhetoric
Generative textile visualization
Live project: acsuarez84.github.io/Image-Analysis-Demo
Fifty photographs — an aquarium, a holiday display, and a pilgrimage through Civil Rights landmarks in Alabama — were opened and described by an AI. It sorted, ranked, and narrated them with total confidence: folding a reflected human face into a whale, deciding what counted as subject versus background.		Live project: acsuarez84.github.io/AgenticDemoing
Compares two ways of encoding the same historical textile texts (Taylor 1909, Gilroy 1845, Rock 1870, and a 1914 home-economics manual): each word's real byte-position in the source versus its ASCII code. Words never written in the sources — like “erased” or “silenced” — carry a value of zero.
A draggable argument-mapping tool lets you tag each image with a lens, connect images to passages from three assigned feminist readings, and build the connections into an actual thesis — argument-as-interface, not argument-as-essay.Those two encodings drive the actual shape of a generated textile pattern — ring spacing, spiral tightness, wave frequency — so presence and erasure in the historical record become something you can see woven, not just read about.
Angely Suarez DeJesus · Text & Technology PhD Student, University of Central Florida
