Mapping What AI Assumes: Patterns and Blind Spots in AI-Generated Text

DueWeek 3 — Friday 9/8, 11:59pm (submitted via Web Courses)
FormatClaude Artifact with a short analysis (word count set by you)
Weight5% of final grade (50 pts total)
Artifact Claude-generated
What You Turn InIn the discussion post:
Your chosen identity markers listed at the top (5 pts)
Your prompt list, 10–20 prompts (10 pts)
Your textual map (10 pts)
Your written reflection (15 pts)
2 peer responses (10 pts)

Objectives:

  • Analyze how AI portrays people across race, gender, class, and language, and explain how those portrayals shape who gets treated as normal, credible, or true.

Learning Goals:

Use intersectionality to find the patterns buried in AI-generated text: the words that repeat, the tone that shifts, and the assumptions the tool treats as normal.

Compare how the tool describes people when you change one identity marker at a time, and explain why the differences matter.

Build a map of what you found, and use it to make your own argument about what the tool does to identity.

Practice using AI the way a researcher does: to learn how these tools work, where they break down, and what that reveals about who built them.

The Role of AI in This Assignment

This is a short, low-stakes experiment that will help you begin to record your prompts in your journal and continue to compile them throughout the semester. You are going to drop 1 of the prompts into Claude with questions connected to race, gender, language, or another identity marker (1-2 at most) of your choosing. Then you are going to slow down and pay close attention to what it generates back.

Then you are going to ask it to generate a textual map of its own generated outputs. Most people use AI to look for answers; in digital humanities, this assignment asks you to do the opposite: use Claude to look for the following:

Which words does the tool treat as normal or default, and whose history does it seem to know best?

Does Claude treat these markers as separate parts of identity, or as one integrated system?

Whose experience does it center — and whose does it leave out?

You will collect those patterns and turn them into a textual map to find how the trope, linguistic patterns, and historical assumptions are connected and buried within the machine’s algorithm. In the discussion post, you will write an initial response about your findings and then respond to two peers.

Use Disclaimer

AI is an integral part of this course because it is what we are critiquing and directly engaging with, either by observation or direct contact. Hence, it is a required element of use.

AI is permitted for:

Embodying your critique and argument

Designing your final project artifact (mainly)

Generating ideas with teacher permission or input

AI is NOT permitted for:

Supplanting your own thinking power and autonomy in your writing and design

Drafting any portion of your writing responses

The Readings Behind the Assignment

Crenshaw, Kimberlé. “Mapping the Margins: Intersectionality, Identity Politics, and Violence against Women of Color.” Stanford Law Review, vol. 43, no. 6, 1991, pp. 1241–99, https://doi.org/10.2307/1229039Throughout this reading, Crenshaw points out that identity is not a single note. It is an intersection of experiences. Race, gender, class, language, and others-these markers do not operate separately. Their overlapping, blending, and dance shapes how people are seen, heard, and treated; forming cultural systems of behaviors and perceptions that are often harmful.
When GAI is trained on human language and artifacts within systems of power, these intersections get put in as pieces, some become invisible or othered.
Hence, look at the key questions and use the readings to help inform your observation plus more that you analyze as this work develops.
Bailey, Moya Z. “All the Digital Humanists Are White, All the Nerds Are Men, but Some of Us Are Brave.” Journal of Digital Humanities, vol. 1, no. 1, 2011, https://journalofdigitalhumanities.org/1-1/all-the-digital-humanists-are-white-all-the-nerds-are-men-but-some-of-us-are-brave-by-moya-z-bailey/Bailey addresses the question towards the field of DH: who is here, and whose knowledge counts? While strides have been made to expand the representational scholarship in the field, this paper produces an argument about the consequences of invisibility, reflective of narrowness.
If digital creators have this narrowness and train models with data reflective of this, the tool’s output will continue to form of knowledge. It will address people through stereotypes or not at all.
Cohen, Cathy J., and Sarah J. Jackson. “Ask a Feminist: A Conversation with Cathy J. Cohen on Black Lives Matter, Feminism, and Contemporary Activism.” Signs: Journal of Women in Culture and Society, vol. 41, no. 4, 2016, pp. 775–92, https://doi.org/10.1086/685115Written as an interview between two scholars, Cohen and Jackson discuss how race and gender intersect in movements, in academic, and in everyday life, which is what you dissecting in Claude’s outputs.

Task Breakdown

STEP 1Choose 2-3 identity markers (does not have to be any that pertain to you if you desire so). This is your lens of analysis for the whole assignment
STEP 2Run a series of prompts through Claude (10-20 prompts) and record them in your prompt journal exactly as you wrote them for, they will be useful in your final assignment. Play around with them: change them, combine, and/or write your own. The goal is to get Claude to produce language about identity, specific to you selected markers.
STEP 3Ask Claude to generate a textual map connecting:
Your map can take any form you want: a table, chart, color-coded annotations of the outputs, hand-drawn diagram photographed and inserted, and so on. What matters is that the visual depicts these areas and how they connect within Claude’s responses.
STEP 4In the discussion post, address the following:
Connect an aspect of your map to Crenshaw’s (1991), Cohen and Jackson’s (2016), or Bailey’s (2011) concept of intersectionality and feminist theory in DH.
Did Claude handle overlapping identity categories or did it treat them as separate isolated entities? What was the effect of this in terms of accuracy or representation?
Name something that surprised you about Claude. It could be something it did well, got wrong, a mix, what was absent or present, and so on. Why does it matter to address it as a digital humanities scholar?
